Case Study — District by Zomato / Live Events

Designing a personalised, social-first event discovery experience

Users juggle three or four apps to discover, book, and show up to a live event. I set out to collapse that into a single, personalised, socially-aware flow inside District — and prove which moment in the journey would move bookings the most.

Project
District by Zomato — Live Events
Type
Self-directed, end-to-end (9-week program)
Role
Product Designer & PM
Timeline
9 weeks
Tools
Figma, competitive research toolkit

Focus metric

Increase the frequency of orders in live-event bookings.

The Problem

Booking a live event takes three or four apps

Business goal: increase the frequency of orders in live-event bookings on District by Zomato by making discovery and booking dramatically better for the people most likely to book often.

User problem: live entertainment is growing fast, but the path from "I'd like to do something this weekend" to "I've booked it" is fragmented. Users bounce between discovery (Instagram, friends), booking (BookMyShow), and on-the-day logistics (maps, food). Every hand-off is a drop-off — and a missed booking.

Why it matters: District sits inside the Zomato ecosystem, which already owns dining intent and location data — an advantage no ticketing app has. If discovery and booking felt personal and social, District could turn one-off ticket buyers into repeat bookers.

Competitive Research

Benchmarking against BookMyShow

I mapped both digital alternatives (BookMyShow, Swiggy Scenes, Meetup, Eventbrite, Nearbuy, Facebook Events) and physical ones (venues running their own nights, community centres, local organisers), then narrowed to the benchmark with the richest public footprint: BookMyShow, the dominant event-ticketing player in India.

I walked both apps screen by screen and ran teardown tools (Wayback Machine, SimilarWeb, ad libraries, company blogs) to surface what BookMyShow solves that District doesn't.

The insight

The biggest lever wasn't features District was missing one-by-one; it was the absence of a personalised discovery experience. That reframed the whole project.

  1. Enhanced event discovery & personalisation — recommendations and quick filters cut the effort of finding relevant events
  2. Comprehensive event insights — descriptions, venue info, seating charts, and reviews to decide with confidence
  3. Flexible booking & payment — promo codes, cashback, and multiple payment rails for different budgets
  4. Post-booking convenience — reminders, calendar sync, cancellation policies that add value beyond the ticket

User Research

8 interviews, one clear pattern

I recruited 8 users across genders, age bands (15–45), cities (Bhubaneswar, Delhi, Mumbai, Bangalore, Hyderabad, Kolkata), and levels of event-going experience — deliberately spanning demographics rather than a single archetype.

I ran semi-structured interviews against a 17-question guide covering leisure habits, how they discover events, who they go with, how they book, and their experience of District specifically. Then I summarised every conversation, grouped it into themes, and quantified the pain.

62.5%
felt event information was not enough to decide — missing venue maps, photos, reviews, transparent pricing
50%
felt event options were limited and lacked personalised recommendations
37%
found event discovery itself an issue — couldn't find events matching their interests
25%
felt they weren't getting value for money — wanted discounts and packages

8 participants across cities, ages, and event-going habits

Recruited deliberately across demographics — name, gender, age, and city — rather than a single archetype, to stress-test whether the pain was universal or niche.

Four themes, quantified across the sample

Every transcript was grouped into four themes — Event Discovery, Event Information, User Engagement, Value for Money — then quantified so the loudest complaint could be checked against the data, not just intuition.

Synthesis

Pains, gains, and jobs — traced back to the metric

I distilled the research into a pains/gains/jobs model and traced each item back to the booking-frequency metric.

The insight

Prioritised segment: Young Professionals (22–35, metro cities). A large slice of Zomato's active base, already using it for dining, spending moderately on experiences, going in small groups, deciding on peer recommendations, and responding to personalised, bundled offers — the users most able to increase booking frequency.

Top pains
Limited event options for a location · high prices with no deals · tedious refunds
Top gains
Personalised recommendations · exclusive discounts/bundles · early access
Top jobs
Discover relevant events · book for a group easily · find bundled dining + event offers

Mapping the Opportunity

Seven moments, one bet

I built a journey map for the segment across six stages — Awareness → Consideration → Decision → Booking → Event → Post-Event — capturing what the user does and needs at each.

From the journey I pulled seven distinct moments in time, and for each I wrote the user's top-level "I want…" statement, then broke each into sub-needs. That became an opportunity tree hung off the single business metric.

The insight

I prioritised Exploring Event Details. Rationale: it's where users spend the most decision time, where competitors under-invest (they optimise listing, not the detail page), and where Zomato's discovery muscle and dining data give District a real edge. Fix this moment and you directly lift conversion.

  1. Discovering an event — "I want to find exciting events that match my interests"
  2. Exploring event details — "I need enough info to decide if this is worth attending"
  3. Deciding to book — "I want to be confident before I pay"
  4. Completing the booking — "I want an easy, hassle-free booking"
  5. Preparing for the event — "I want to plan my visit efficiently"
  6. Attending the event — "I want a seamless, enjoyable experience at the venue"
  7. Post-event — "I want to share my experience and find more like it"

Ideation

Three concepts for Exploring Event Details

For the chosen opportunity I generated three solution directions and story-mapped each so the flow was unambiguous.

Interactive & Personalised Event Details Page

Auto-generated takeaways ("90% of attendees loved the vibe"), "people like you also booked this," and a "grab a meal before the event" nudge that leans on Zomato's restaurant graph.

Social Proof & Real-Time Engagement

Live reviews and media from the venue, a friends-attending feature, and an event popularity meter ("500+ booked in the last hour").

Event Q&A & Community Forum

User-generated Q&A, moderator-verified answers, and pre-event threads to coordinate meetups and carpools.

Concept 1 — a personalised, insight-rich detail page

Story-mapped as a user/platform swim-lane to make the flow from landing to decision unambiguous before any UI was drawn.

Concept 2 — FOMO, live reviews, friends attending

Mapped the moments where real-time social signals — who's going, what people are saying right now — could intercept hesitation.

Concept 3 — a community layer that answers pre-booking questions

Mapped how a Q&A thread could resolve the situational questions a static description never anticipates.

Narrowing Down

MoSCoW: two Must-Haves, one supporting layer

The Interactive Detail Page and the Q&A/Community layer both scored Must-Have — they remove uncertainty at the exact moment users decide, and they reuse District's existing recommendation and filtering logic for a fast rollout. Social proof landed as a strong Should-Have and folded into the winning direction as a supporting layer.

Prioritising against user expectation and feasibility

Each idea scored against user expectation, business value, and build feasibility — the table that turned three concepts into one shippable direction.

The Solution

An interactive, personalised event detail page with a live social layer

It leans on AI-driven recommendations, real-time engagement, and Zomato's dining and location data to make the deciding moment effortless and confident.

Personalised event discovery

Recommendations from preferences, past bookings, and browsing; segmented into trending, nearby, and exclusive deals.

Rich detail page

Full venue info, reviews and photos, live ratings, and an Event Q&A/community forum.

Social proof & engagement

Friends-attending, a live popularity meter, celebrities attending, and shareable highlights.

Seamless booking

Multiple payment options with card/bank discounts, and an easy refund/cancellation path.

Post-event loop

Feedback capture that feeds the next round of recommendations.

Rollout & Risk

A phased launch, planned for what could go wrong

Phased launch: beta to 5–10% of metro users (Month 1–2) → enhancement and A/B testing at 30–40% (Month 3–4) → full launch with marketing (Month 5) → optimise and expand partnerships (Month 6+).

Risks planned for: thin event inventory (mitigated with organiser partnerships), payment drop-off (retries, alternative rails, support), and security (encryption, 2FA, monitoring). Edge cases considered: first-time users with no history, payment failures, cancellations, and non-metro/pin-code users.

Measuring Success

Instrumented before it shipped, not after

I defined the analytics up front so the feature could prove itself. I specified a full event-tracking spec across three phases — Discovery & Exploration, Social Engagement, and Event Experience — each event with its trigger, event properties, and user properties (e.g. event_viewed, event_filtered, friends_attending_viewed, event_checkin_completed, event_feedback_submitted).

Success
Daily active users · average order value · preferences set · wishlists created
Failure
High drop-off · falling engagement · fewer bookings per user despite rollout

What I Took Away

Three things I'd carry into the next project

  1. The metric is the compass — anchoring every pain, gain, and idea to booking frequency stopped the project sprawling into a feature wishlist
  2. Prioritising a moment beats prioritising a feature — betting on "Exploring Event Details" gave the whole solution a spine
  3. Use the ecosystem you're in — District's edge was never going to be out-listing BookMyShow; it was dining data, location, and social graph, and the solution leans hard on that
